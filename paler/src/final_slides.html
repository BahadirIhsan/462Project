Problem and question

  • A password can be stolen; how it is typed (hold times, latencies) is harder to copy → a free second factor, no extra hardware.
  • Static verification: everyone types the same password .tie5Roanl; a per-user detector accepts or rejects one entry.
  • Typing rhythm drifts from day to day → within-session results can be optimistic.
  • Question (A5): how well do simple and learned detectors separate users, and how much do they lose across sessions?
Since the checkpoint: C1/C2 results with CIs · template-ageing curve · Siamese embedding (cross-fitted) · detector and multi-entry fusion · error analysis · login demo.

Learned models: best today, worst tomorrow

  • Siamese is the best detector within a session: 10.6% vs. 12.2% (scaled Manhattan).
  • Across sessions it falls to 15.7%: the largest loss of all detectors (+5.1 pp, CI [3.2, 7.6]).
  • Scaled Manhattan also loses (+2.8 pp) although it has 8× more enrolment data in C2.
  • Mahalanobis improves C1→C2: with 25 entries its covariance is the bottleneck, not drift.
  • C2: scaled Manhattan, Mahalanobis, SVM and Siamese are statistically tied (~14–16%).
Within-session numbers are optimistic, most of all for the learned embedding.

Genuine scores of later sessions move right of the enrolment mean (0): that is drift. Overlap with impostors is large.

Template ageing and real thresholds

  • Enrolment fixed at 25 entries of session 1: EER grows with the gap, e.g. scaled Manhattan 12.9% → 24.1%, Siamese 14.3% → 29.4%.
  • Threshold from enrolment only (95th percentile of own held-out scores), applied to sessions 5–8, scaled Manhattan: FNMR 2.6% but FMR 55.6%.
  • At FMR 1% about half of genuine entries are rejected.
A deployed system must re-enrol / update the template, or accept many false matches.

What helps: more evidence, not more model

Colours: ■ Manhattan ■ scaled Manhattan ■ Mahalanobis ■ one-class SVM ■ Siamese ■ fusion SM+SVM+Siamese

  • Detector fusion (SM + SVM + Siamese, z-norm from enrolment): C2 12.7% (vs. 15.0%; CI of the gain includes 0). Detectors fail on the same users (Spearman 0.97).
  • Multi-entry fusion (average k consecutive entries): k=5 → 7.1%, k=10 → 6.1%.
  • Multi-session enrolment: C2 with sessions 1–4 beats the 25-entry ageing curve at every later session.
Practical design: one entry normally, ask for another only when the score is borderline (step-up).

Error analysis

  • Few users dominate: 8/41 below 5% EER, 6/41 above 25%; the 5 worst give 28% of the total error (hard for every detector, "goats").
  • Wolves: median impostor accepted 11% at the EER threshold, but s026 52% and s029 45%.
  • Features: hold times drift most (0.48 SD vs. 0.34 DD); biggest shift at e→5 (letter → digit row). All 41 users got faster.
  • Drift size vs. per-user EER: ρ = 0.24 (p = 0.13), not significant → hard users are also inconsistent or similar to others.
  • Enrolment-only rejection of slow entries: ≤ 0.25 pp gain.

Demo: RhythmGate, password + rhythm login

  • Flask + SQLite, runs on localhost; browser records key-down/up times; same feature code (CMU rows replayed as key events → identical vectors, unit-tested).
  • Register → enrol ≥ 15 entries (2 sessions better) → log in. Typos / Backspace discard the entry, as in CMU.
  • Decision = exactly the report's rule: SM + SVM (+ Siamese for the CMU password), z-norm and threshold from own held-out entries; 3 security levels; k = 1 or 3 entries.
  • Storage: password only as salted scrypt hash; template = timing numbers, no key text; local only; "delete my data" (cascade).
  • Impostor-test mode: a team-mate types my password; logged, never signs in. Own demo data: team members only, collected with the instructor's approval, never used in the evaluation.

Limitations and conclusions

Limitations

  • 41 subjects → CIs of about ±3 pp; small differences unresolved.
  • One password, lab keyboard; impostors did not practise the victim's rhythm.
  • Drift partly = learning a new password.
  • Small Siamese MLP, ≈43 training subjects, no session-aware training.
  • Demo: browser timing, few trials, template stored unprotected.

Conclusions

  • Reference reproduced (scaled Manhattan 9.6%).
  • Cross-session: best single detectors ≈ 14–15% EER, statistically tied.
  • Learned embedding: best within session, largest cross-session loss.
  • Error grows with time; enrolment thresholds transfer poorly.
  • Most effective: multi-session enrolment and multi-entry fusion (≈ 7% at k = 5).
Keystroke rhythm is a cheap, useful second factor, not a stand-alone authenticator.
